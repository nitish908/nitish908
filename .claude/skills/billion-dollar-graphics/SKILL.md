# Billion Dollar Graphics

Source: Mike Parkinson, *Do-It-Yourself Billion Dollar Graphics* (PepperLip Press). Parkinson built this methodology creating high-stakes proposal and sales graphics for defense/government contractors (Lockheed Martin, Raytheon, Northrop Grumman, NSA, and others), where graphic quality measurably moves win rates. The core insight: a business graphic almost never exists just to display information — it exists to get a specific audience to believe or do something. Every principle below serves that goal.

Why this matters mechanically: visuals are processed faster than text and land in long-term memory more readily, while text is bottlenecked by short-term memory (roughly 7±2 items). Visuals also trigger an emotional response before the viewer consciously evaluates the content, and emotion measurably shapes what people decide. So a graphic operates on two tracks at once — a **surface (cognitive)** track that communicates facts, and a **subsurface (emotional)** track that shapes how those facts feel. Good persuasive graphics are deliberate about both tracks, not just the first one.

## Before designing anything: know the P.A.Q.S.

Do not jump to layout or chart-type choice until you can answer these four questions — Parkinson calls getting this right "50% of the conceptualization process." If the user hasn't given you enough to answer them, ask before designing. A graphic built without clear P.A.Q.S. is guessing.

- **P — Primary Objective.** What should the audience believe or do after seeing this graphic? Push past a feature-level answer to a benefit-level one. "Show our new network architecture" is a bad primary objective; "lower cost, faster speed, and greater uptime through our three-step network approach" is a good one, because it leads with the audience's payoff and only then explains how. Ask yourself "so what — why does the audience care?" until you get there. If several benefits compete for space, don't pile them all in: one clear benefit lands harder than three diluted ones.
- **A — Audience.** Who exactly is looking at this, what do they already know, what do they want, and what would make them defensive or skeptical? A graphic aimed at a technical evaluator and one aimed at an executive skimming a deck should rarely look identical even if the underlying data is the same.
- **Q — Questions.** What does this audience need answered to move toward the primary objective — their who/what/where/when/why/how? Put yourself in their seat and make the answer obvious rather than implied.
- **S — Subject Matter.** Do you (or the user) actually understand the underlying content well enough to represent it accurately? A graphic can't clarify a subject the author doesn't understand.

If the user's request is underspecified on any of these (especially P and A), ask a short clarifying question rather than guessing — the rest of the process depends on getting this right first.

## The 3-step process

**1. Know the P.A.Q.S.** (above).

**2. Sketch / conceptualize.** Turn the P.A.Q.S. answers into a visual concept before worrying about polish. Four conceptualization methods, useful in different situations:
   - **Literal** — show exactly what's described. Use when the content is already concrete (a floor plan, a physical object, an org's actual structure).
   - **Substitution** — replace the concept with a visual metaphor, analogy, or simile that shares the relevant characteristics. Use for abstract ideas that have no natural picture (e.g., "our support process" as a road/bridge graphic, "protection" as a dome graphic). Pick a substitution the audience will actually recognize — an obscure metaphor adds a decoding step and works against you.
   - **Quantitative** — the content is fundamentally numeric (values, amounts, time); choose a chart type that matches the relationship in the data (see the graphic-type catalog below).
   - **Assembly** — build the graphic from smaller information chunks (building blocks) that combine to show a relationship or interaction, useful when several distinct elements need to be shown working together.

   These aren't mutually exclusive — a single graphic (e.g., a dashboard) often assembles multiple quantitative elements, or pairs a substitution metaphor with literal labels.

**3. Render.** Execute cleanly: accurate, uncluttered, and stylistically consistent with both the subject matter and the audience's expectations (a defense-industry proposal graphic and a consumer app's onboarding graphic should not share a visual register). This is where you'd hand off to canvas-design, dataviz, or another rendering skill/tool — but keep steps 1-2's decisions driving the rendering rather than starting from an aesthetic template.

## Rules to check before calling a graphic done

- **Ockham's Razor: simplicity beats complexity.** If a simpler version communicates the same primary objective, use it. Complexity that isn't earning its place is working against the audience, not for them.
- **The 10-Second Rule.** The audience must grasp the main point within about 10 seconds, or the graphic has failed regardless of how accurate or detailed it is. After drafting, ask: could someone seeing this cold state the primary objective back in 10 seconds? If not, cut or restructure until they can.
- **Avoid visual noise.** Too many competing elements, textures, or colors read as clutter and induce a negative reaction to the subject matter, independent of whether the underlying content is sound. Every element should be there because it serves the primary objective or the audience's comprehension — not because it's available.
- **Be deliberate about the subsurface (emotional) layer, not just the surface (cognitive) one.** Color, imagery, and composition shape mood and trust before the viewer processes a single fact. When critiquing a graphic that's technically correct but "not landing," check the emotional register first — a cluttered, cold, or inconsistent graphic can undercut a perfectly accurate one.

## Choosing the right graphic type

Once you know the relationship you're showing (comparison, trend over time, hierarchy, process/flow, composition/parts-of-whole, or a physical/spatial relationship), pick the graphic type built for that relationship rather than defaulting to whatever's easiest to produce. See `references/graphic-types.md` for the full catalog with one-line definitions of each type (bar/line/bubble/pie chart, Gantt, waterfall, funnel, org chart, Venn diagram, timeline, dashboard, cutaway/exploded diagram, process/flow diagram, pyramid, road/bridge graphic, and more) — read it when you need to match content to a specific graphic type or want to suggest alternatives during a critique.

## Applying this to a critique (not just a fresh design)

When asked to review or improve an existing graphic rather than create one from scratch, work backward through the same framework:
1. Infer or ask for the primary objective and audience — most weak graphics fail because these were never nailed down, not because of a rendering flaw.
2. Check whether the graphic actually leads with a benefit (if persuasive) or a clear fact (if informational), and whether it passes the 10-second test.
3. Check for visual noise and unclear substitution metaphors.
4. Only then critique rendering polish (color, alignment, type).

This order matters — a beautifully rendered graphic with the wrong primary objective is a bigger problem than a plain graphic with the right one.
